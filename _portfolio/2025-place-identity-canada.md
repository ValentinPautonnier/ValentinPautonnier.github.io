Canada's rural-urban electoral divide has widened over the past two decades, even though rural and urban Canadians do not disagree more on policy than they used to. Using Canadian Election Study (CES) data from 2000 to 2025, this paper first traces how much the urbanity of a riding shapes vote choice, and then uses new measures of **place-based identity** and **place-based resentment**, fielded in the 2025 CES, to examine the mechanisms behind the divide.

The main finding is that place-based consciousness is not a single force favouring the right. Resentment pushes voters toward the Conservatives everywhere, but more strongly in rural areas. Identity, by contrast, follows the content of the place one identifies with: a strong rural identity increases Conservative voting, while a strong urban identity reduces it.

## A sudden break in 2019

![](/images/URBAN_VOTE_ALL_PROVFE.png)

*Effect of riding urbanity (0 = least urban, 1 = most urban) on voting Conservative (navy circles), Liberal (red diamonds) and NDP (orange triangles), by election. Hollow markers include province fixed effects only; filled markers add socio-demographic controls. Bars are 95% confidence intervals.*

After a decade of stability, the effect of living in an urban riding jumps in 2019 and keeps growing through 2025: in the latest election, moving from the least to the most urban riding reduces the probability of voting Conservative by about 25 points and raises the probability of voting Liberal by about 26 points. The divide runs mainly between Liberals and Conservatives; the NDP gains little from urban voters. Just as important, the gap between hollow and filled markers shrinks over time: before 2019, part of the urban-rural gap reflected who lived where; since then, place predicts vote choice net of residents' characteristics.

## Identity and resentment work differently

![](/images/coefplot_rural_suburban_urban.png)

*Coefficients from separate models of Conservative voting in 2025 among respondents who identify their place as rural (hollow circles), suburban (triangles) or urban (filled circles). Horizontal lines are 95% confidence intervals.*

Place-based resentment predicts Conservative voting in all three groups, but the effect is about 70% larger among rural identifiers. Place-based identity has the opposite pattern: strongly positive among rural identifiers, null among suburban identifiers, and strongly negative among urban identifiers.

## Resentment: same direction, steeper slope in rural Canada

![](/images/margins_pbr_r_u.png)

*Predicted probability of voting Conservative by level of place-based resentment, for rural (light dashed) and urban (dark dash-dot) identifiers, with 95% confidence intervals.*

At low levels of resentment, rural and urban identifiers are equally (un)likely to vote Conservative. As resentment rises, both curves go up, but the rural one climbs much faster: at maximum resentment, 61% of rural identifiers vote Conservative, against 47% of urban identifiers.

## Identity: opposite effects for rural and urban Canadians

![](/images/images/margins_pbi_r_u.png)

*Predicted probability of voting Conservative by strength of place-based identity, for rural and urban identifiers.*

This is the central result. Among rural identifiers, a stronger attachment to one's place raises Conservative support from around 15% to 56%. Among urban identifiers, the same attachment lowers it from around 52% to 20%. The two lines cross at mid-level identity. These effects survive controls for prior vote and party identification: rural identity predicts defection *to* the Conservatives among 2021 Liberal and NDP voters, and urban identity predicts defection *from* the Conservatives even among 2021 Conservative voters.
